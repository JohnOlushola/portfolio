New idea, new interface. That is the reflex. A fresh Notion workspace, a little app, a shiny site nobody asked for. It used to belong to engineers. Now that code is cheap it belongs to everyone who can vibe code their way to a working thing.

I think that reflex is mostly wrong now.

Here is what provovked the thinking for me. I wanted to track my lifts and actually progress them week to week. The old me builds a workout app. Schema, screens, a chart, the works. Code is cheap now, isn't it. Instead I log the numbers in the notes app on my phone between sets, and the intelligence reads that note, works out what I lifted last week, and tells me what to beat. No app. No new surface to learn. The note was already there. I just connected something smart to it.

That is the whole idea.

## The interface was never the product

Two things first, because I will be sloppy with the word otherwise. An interface is not a UI. A UI is one kind of interface, the kind with screens and buttons and a design system behind it. An interface is just the surface where intent meets a system. A sentence can be one. A note can be one. A camera can be one. So when I say fewer interfaces I do not mean fewer ways in. I mean fewer new surfaces you have to learn before you get what you came for.

Because so far the answer to a new idea has been a new screen. There is a flow to getting the result out of the app. A feature ships, so you make sure people can find it, reach it, understand it. You design the product around the fact that the feature has to be visible.

Take a couple of steps back though. Nobody wakes up wanting a workout tracking app. They want to know if they are getting stronger or leaving gains on the table, whether they are recovering properly, what to do today. Nobody wants a project management tool either. They want the thing to move forward and the team to stay in step. I do not think ants get on a call every Monday to align on objectives, and they manage.

Strip it back and most of what we call products are three things. A connection to where your stuff already lives. Some context about you and what you want. A result handed back. Everything else is scaffolding. Process, code, systems we built to deliver those three things under a constraint that was mostly about interfaces.

## Less is more?

So maybe you do not need Notion to run your life. Maybe your calendar is enough. Maybe your notes app is enough. Maybe the surfaces you already open every day are enough, and the thing that was missing was never another app. It was intelligence wired into the ones you have.

_Book a meeting with Joe._ I do not open a scheduling tool, hunt for a slot, type it in, send it off. I say the sentence. It happens. It hands me back a link. The interface collapses down to intent in, result out.

The part that is easy to miss is what this does to building. To make a new product I might not write software at all in the old sense. No front end, no screens, no design system. What I create is the logic. The context and the rules the intelligence uses to produce the result. The product becomes the thinking, not the thing you click.

And the logic is personal. It moves when I move. I do not file a request and wait for someone to enable the feature I need, I change the rules. That is the bit I keep circling back to. If an interface is the surface where intent meets a system, then the rules I write are the interface now. It just stopped looking like a screen.

## Interfaces still have to be earned

Here is the honest other half, because there is a lazy version of this argument I do not believe.

I am not saying hand your thinking to a model and go to sleep. I am not saying chat is the answer to everything, because it is not. Interfaces are not friction by default. Sometimes the interface is the best tool for the job and no amount of intelligence changes that.

Your eyes are parallel. Language is serial. When you need to scan, compare, and catch the odd one out, a good visual beats a paragraph every time. A week laid out in a grid. A chart. A map. A table where the outlier jumps at you. Try saying all of that in a sentence and you lose the thing that made it useful.

Then there is touch. Anything spatial or precise wants your hands, not your words. Dragging, cropping, arranging, drawing, nudging something two pixels to the left. _Move it a bit that way_ will always lose to just moving it.

And there is discovery. When you do not yet know what you want, an interface shows you. It offers the options, teaches you what is possible, gives you edges to push against. A blank text box does none of that. It assumes you already hold the whole idea in your head, and often you do not.

There is a heavier version of this too, and Chris Gregori puts it well: [code is cheap now, software isn't](https://www.chrisgregori.dev/opinion/code-is-cheap-now-software-isnt). Generating the thing is trivial. Keeping it alive is not. Maintenance, edge cases, data ownership, security, compliance, support, the unglamorous work of getting anyone to care. A note and some rules survive none of that. The moment other people depend on it, the moment it has to answer to a customer or a regulator or a bank quietly changing its export format, you are back in software, and software still costs what it always cost.

Which is the same thing I was getting at in [Craft still wins](/notes/craft-still-wins). The tools moved the cheap part. They did not move the hard part, and they did not hand anybody judgement.

So the rule is not fewer interfaces for their own sake. It is earn the interface. Build the screen where sight, touch, precision, or discovery genuinely beat a plain result, or where the thing has to hold up for other people over time. Everywhere else, connect the intelligence and skip it.

Take a picture of the receipt. Ask the intelligence to read it, generate the invoice, and leave a draft email sitting in your outbox. That never needed an app. Most things do not.

## Where I have landed

The old cost of a new idea was heavy. Design it, build the interface, ship the app, get people to learn it. That is a lot of friction, and most ideas die in it.

The new cost is close to nothing. A connection. Some context. A result. The idea does not need another interface. It needs a way in and a way out.

The people who feel this early stop asking what should I build, and start asking what already holds this, and what should I connect to it. Fewer interfaces. More done.

----

And, of course. This whole thing, the plan for my week, the reading behind this piece, the log that reads my lifts back to me, was made exactly this way. No new app. Just my calendar, a note, and something smart connected to them.

Maybe that is the point.

Peace.
