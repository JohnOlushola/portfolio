I wanted to track my lifts and actually progress them week to week.

The old me goes looking for an app that meets the need. Search, install three, hate the onboarding on all of them, settle for the least annoying one and stop opening it by week three.

The more recent me thinks, code is cheap now, I will just build my own. Personalised. My schema, my chart, progressive overload baked in.

The current me thinks, I do not need another app. Fine. I will log it in my notes.

So that is what I did. I write the numbers into a note on my phone between sets, and the intelligence reads that note, works out what I lifted last week, and tells me what to beat. No app. No new surface to learn. The note was already there. I just connected something smart to it.

Three things pushed me there.

Constraint. Not having the means used to shape the answer. You could not build everything, so you picked, and picking made the thing sharper.

Saturation. Everything already exists. Another app is another login, another place to check, another thing to abandon.

Simplicity. The plainest version of this was always going to win. A table in a note is legible to me in a way a dashboard never was.

And here is the part I keep sitting with. Now that the cost of writing code has approached zero, we have lost the features of friction. Friction used to do work for us. It filtered ideas, killed the weak ones early, and forced you to be sure before you committed. Nothing filters now. You can have the app by lunch, which means you will build the app by lunch, and you will never find out whether you needed it.

So knowing what not to build has become at least as important as knowing what to build. Maybe more. That is the point where [taste and craft](/notes/craft-still-wins) start to matter again, because when the constraint is gone, judgement is the only thing left doing the filtering.

## What's the interface?

An interface is not a UI. A UI is one kind of interface, the one with screens and buttons and a design system behind it. An interface is the surface where intent meets a system. An API is one. A piece of hardware is one. A sentence, a note, a camera, a phone call. Fewer interfaces does not mean fewer ways in. It means fewer new surfaces to learn before you get what you came for.

And look at what we optimised those surfaces for. Fixed. Predictable. Scalable. That was the right trade when software was expensive, because you were building one thing for a lot of people and it had to hold still long enough to be understood. But it was a trade. What we paid was personalisation, fun, personality. Everyone gets the same screen, and the screen is designed for the average of everyone, which is nobody.

The constraint that justified the trade is gone. So it is worth asking what we would build if we were not still paying for it.

Maybe some solutions do not need to exist anymore. This is the quiet version of the SaaSpocalypse people keep predicting. Not that the software gets replaced by better software, but that the problem stops being worth a product.

Maybe some solutions stop being hosted and belong to the user. Your rules, your data, running where you are, rather than a seat you rent in someone else's system.

And maybe the solution is your calendar. A phone call. A text message. A picture.

Nobody wakes up wanting a workout tracking app. They want to know if they are getting stronger or leaving gains on the table. Nobody wants a project management tool either, they want the thing to move forward and the team to stay in step. I do not think ants get on a call every Monday to align on objectives, and they manage (reductive? I know haha).

Strip it back and most of what we call products are three things. A connection to where your stuff already lives. Some context about you and what you want. A result handed back. Everything else is scaffolding we built because the answer to a new idea was always a new screen.

## Less is more?

So maybe you do not need Notion to run your life. Maybe your calendar is enough, and what was missing was intelligence wired into the surfaces you already open.

_Book a meeting with Joe._ I do not open a scheduling tool, hunt for a slot, type it in, send it off. I say the sentence. It happens. It hands me back a link. Intent in, result out.

What this does to building is easy to miss. To make a new product I might not write software at all in the old sense. No front end, no screens, no design system. What I create is the logic, the context and rules the intelligence uses to produce the result. The product becomes the thinking, not the thing you click.

And the logic is personal. It moves when I move. I do not wait for someone to enable a feature, I change the rules. If an interface is the surface where intent meets a system, then the rules I write are the interface now. It just stopped looking like a screen.

## Interfaces still have to be earned

Here is the honest other half. I am not saying hand your thinking to a model and go to sleep, or that chat is the answer to everything. Interfaces are not friction by default. Sometimes the interface is the best tool for the job and no amount of intelligence changes that.

Your eyes are parallel. Language is serial. When you need to scan, compare, and catch the odd one out, a good visual beats a paragraph every time. A week in a grid. A table where the outlier jumps at you. Try saying that in a sentence and you lose what made it useful.

Then there is touch. Anything spatial or precise wants your hands, not your words. Dragging, cropping, arranging, nudging something two pixels to the left. _Move it a bit that way_ will always lose to just moving it.

And there is discovery. When you do not yet know what you want, an interface shows you. It offers options, and edges to push against. A blank text box assumes you already hold the whole idea in your head, and often you do not.

Chris Gregori puts the heavier version well: [code is cheap now, software isn't](https://www.chrisgregori.dev/opinion/code-is-cheap-now-software-isnt). Generating the thing is trivial, keeping it alive is not. Maintenance, edge cases, ownership, compliance, the unglamorous work of getting anyone to care. The moment other people depend on it, or it has to answer to a bank quietly changing its export format, you are back in software. Same point I made in [Craft still wins](/notes/craft-still-wins): the tools moved the cheap part, not the hard part.

So the rule is not fewer interfaces for their own sake. It is earn the interface. Build the screen where those genuinely beat a plain result, or where the thing has to hold up for other people over time. Everywhere else, connect the intelligence and skip it. Take a picture of the receipt, ask for the invoice, get a draft email back. That never needed an app. Most things do not.

## Where I have landed

The old cost of a new idea was heavy. Design it, build it, ship it, get people to learn it. Most ideas die in that friction.

The new cost is close to nothing. A connection, some context, a result. So the question stops being what should I build, but what already holds this, and what do I connect to it. Fewer interfaces. More done.

----

And, of course. This whole thing, the plan for my week, the reading behind this piece, the log that reads my lifts back to me, was made exactly this way. No new app. Just my calendar, a note, and something smart connected to them.

Maybe that is the point.

Peace.
